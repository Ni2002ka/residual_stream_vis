How to read the grid

Columns are tokens and rows are layers: the input at the bottom, the model's output at the top. Each cell is the model's state for that token at that layer, labeled with the word the lens reads there. Click a cell (or use the arrow keys) to see what flowed into it. Curved lines are attention bringing information from earlier tokens; the straight line is what the token kept from itself.

Let the model answer makes it write a response (step by step, if you ask), and the grid covers your text and its response. Highlight a word colors every cell by how highly the lens ranks that word.

What the word in a cell means

The model turns a vector into words only once, at the very end: it takes the top cell of a column, applies a final normalization, and multiplies by the unembedding, a matrix with one row per vocabulary word. That gives every word a score (the logits), and the highest is the predicted next word. Every column does this for the word that follows it, which is why the top row reads one word ahead (the el column says Tower).

A lens borrows that final step and uses it on a middle cell, as if the model had stopped there and had to answer. So the word in a cell answers one question: "if the model had to say the next word from this vector, which word would it pick?" It is not a description of everything in the vector.

Logit lens, J-lens and the model

The residual stream is a running sum: each layer adds to it and nothing is overwritten.

top cell  =  cell at layer l  +  everything layers l+1 … top add
What it decodesWhat it assumes
Logit lensthe cell at layer lthe layers above add nothing that matters (drop the second term)
J-lensJ · cellthe layers above respond to this cell in their average, linear way
The modelthe top cellnothing: the real layers ran

The logit lens is the J-lens with J set to the identity matrix, and at the top layer all three are the same. The logit lens works at all because every layer writes into the same space, so once a middle layer has written "Paris" into a cell it can be read straight away. It fails early because early cells mostly hold things meant for later layers (which token this is, its position, grammar), not finished predictions.

What the J-lens leaves out

  • It's an average. J was measured once, on ordinary web text: how the top row moves when this layer's cell is nudged. It isn't this prompt's computation.
  • It's linear. One matrix stands in for many nonlinear layers, like a tangent line standing in for a curve.
  • It only carries what's already in the cell. The real layers above keep pulling in information from other tokens; the J-lens can't add what isn't there yet. In the Eiffel prompt it reads Constantinople at layer 8 ("a city goes here"); Paris only arrives once later layers bring in more from the "Eiffel Tower" columns.

To see real propagation instead of an estimate, edit a cell and rerun: that runs every layer above.

Which direction to edit with

Every word has two directions you can push a cell along: its logit-lens direction (its row of the unembedding) and its J-lens direction (that row pulled back through J). They're good at different edits:

EditLogit-lens directionJ-lens direction
Push the last token of the Eiffel prompt toward London (Gemma, layer 8)London 100%London 0%
Same, GPT-2, layer 6London 100%London 11%
Swap the animal at its own token in "How many legs does a … have?" (Gemma, 24 pairs of animals it answers correctly, layer 6)1 / 24 give the new animal's count6 / 24
Same, layer 80 / 245 / 24
Same, 80 pairs, layer 8 (experiments/animal_swap.py)0 / 8032 / 80 (random directions: 1 / 80)

The reason is in how J is measured: it adds up a cell's effect on the top row at its own position and at every later position. So a J-lens direction means "make the rest of the computation treat this as word X". That's what you want when the word is a concept other tokens read, like the animal the question is about. To make this token output a word, the plain unembedding direction is better: the residual connections carry it to the top unchanged.

Reading a chain of thought

With an instruction-tuned model (for example google/gemma-3-4b-it), choose Generate a response: the model answers your message step by step, and the grid covers your message and its whole response (tinted). Then track the answer word to see every cell where a lens ranks it highly. Cells to the left of where the model first writes the answer show what it was already working toward.

On Gemma-3-4B-it, for the Eiffel Tower, aunt and ball-in-box examples, both lenses rank the answer #1 at the newline that closes the question, around layer 26 of 34, before the model writes any of its steps. For "who is the shortest?" it doesn't appear until just before it's written. Two cautions: a readout shows what the model is disposed to say, not proof that the written steps are unused (edit a step and rerun to test that), and track one-token words (names usually are; Gemma writes numbers digit by digit).

Why the bottom rows show odd words

  • Layer 0 is just the input. Each bottom cell is its token's embedding plus a position embedding; no block has run and no information has moved between tokens. There is no next-word prediction in it yet, but a lens always prints its best guess anyway.
  • The guesses there are leftovers. Words like theless (the tail of "nevertheless"), ModLoader (a Minecraft tool's name) or tremend are rarely-predicted tokens whose vectors sit near the common, average direction most cells share. When there's no real prediction, they win. In GPT-2, ModLoader is the J-lens's top word for about a quarter of ordinary tokens at layer 1 and none from layer 7 up.
  • A high percentage doesn't make it real. theless gets 54% on the first token, which only means one junk word beat the others. Trust a readout once it's a plausible next word and holds steady over a few layers.
  • Information only moves left to right. Attention lets a cell pull from earlier tokens, never later ones, so the first column only ever sees itself. Models often park attention there (the "attention sink").
